# Heat Signal Final Reflection

Heat Signal is an interactive dashboard about 264,750 NYC 311 HEAT/HOT WATER reports filed during 2024. I designed it to help viewers investigate when reports rise, how the pattern differs by borough, how reported scope changes with a selection, and when reports concentrate during the week. The central limitation remains visible throughout the dashboard: a 311 report is a signal from a resident, not a verified heating failure, prevalence estimate, or borough risk measure.

**Cleveland and McGill effectiveness.** The main comparisons use aligned position and length rather than area, angle, or color. The monthly view places time in chronological left-to-right position and uses stacked bar length for total report volume. The borough view uses a common baseline and bar length for reports per 10,000 renter-occupied homes. This change addresses the problem with raw borough totals: a larger borough can have more reports simply because it contains more renter households. Raw counts remain in the hover detail so the rate is interpretable rather than hiding the underlying volume. The heatmap reserves color lightness for a compact matrix where 168 separate bars would be difficult to scan.

**Expressiveness and perceptual color design.** Every channel matches the data type. Month, weekday, and hour are ordered variables and use ordered positions. Borough and reported scope are nominal variables and use categorical labels. Report count and the normalized report rate are quantitative and use length or lightness. I deliberately separated the two categorical palettes: stable, colorblind-considered borough hues identify the stacked monthly segments, while the two scope categories use neutral dark and light gray. This prevents the earlier ambiguity where a teal or orange mark meant a borough in one view and a scope category in another. The heatmap uses one constant orange hue whose lightness moves from very light for lower counts to dark for higher counts. This is sequential rather than diverging because the data have no meaningful midpoint. The adjacent legend is recalculated with the same values and units as the cells, so the heatmap, hover labels, and KPI agree.

**Gestalt grouping and visual hierarchy.** The page follows overview, comparison, then detail. A compact hero establishes the topic and caveat without pushing the first chart below the fold. The current-selection strip and KPI cards are enclosed summary groups. The monthly overview occupies the first full-width panel because seasonality is the entry point to the story. Borough and reported-scope charts are paired by proximity because they are both categorical comparisons, but their headings and palettes distinguish their different meanings. The wide weekday-hour heatmap appears after those comparisons because it needs the most horizontal space and works as a drill-down view. Consistent panel borders, spacing, typography, and numbered sections use similarity and enclosure to show what belongs together.

**Data-ink ratio and interaction tradeoffs.** The dashboard favors data marks, direct labels, concise explanatory text, and hover details over decorative chart furniture. The prominent exception is the introductory illustration, which functions as editorial framing and is intentionally shorter in the final version. Cross-filtering is the primary interaction: selecting a borough, month, scope, or weekday-hour cell updates the KPI strip and every coordinated view. Filter chips make the state visible, and Clear all returns to the full extract. RQ3 is no longer left for the viewer to infer: the scope panel explicitly states which reported scope is more common and its share in the current selection. The rate comparison is also framed carefully; it improves comparability but does not make a causal or diagnostic claim.

The final design therefore prioritizes accurate visual semantics over visual novelty. It gives the viewer an accessible overview first, retains exact values on demand, and makes both the analytical answers and the limitations clear.
